Refining the Intial Experience
of the au Tsukasapo app

Improving the UX was a long-term roadmap initiative, backed by user data and ongoing qualitative research.

Outcome

While this case study doesn’t track business metrics, it reflects a clear belief in how product improvements should be made: through insight-led design, sprint-based refinement, and a commitment to continuously improve.

In past roles, I worked in agile teams that moved quickly and improved through frequent releases. In contrast, my current organization has strong structure—roadmaps, planning, and testing are all well defined—but at times struggles to deliver meaningful change.

Good product development isn’t about finding the perfect solution up front—it’s about acting on strong signals and taking a well-informed first step. Insight gives you direction, but real value comes from putting ideas into motion and refining them through sprints. Momentum matters.

Decisiveness

Progress starts by moving. It's better to ship, learn, and adjust than to stall waiting for certainty.

Testing

Usability testing gives fast, practical validation—and the confidence to act.

Continuous Improvement

When you expect to iterate, it’s easier to move forward without hesitation.

Background

A Rich Support App for Senior Users

The au Tsukasapo app is a support application with a rich set of features that help users solve smartphone problems, access curated content, and improve device literacy. The service targets low- to mid-literacy users, often overlapping with senior demographics—a large and important user group in Japan.

The service has over five million paid subscribers, and consistent data reports highlighted three critical areas for improvement: completing the provisioning flow, sustaining engagement after the first week, and helping users better understand the app’s features and purpose.

A Refined Process and Deep Knowledge

I worked on the Tsukasapo app for approximately four years. During this time, we operated with a well-defined process—structured roadmaps, sprint planning with cross-functional alignment, and continuous usability testing conducted roughly every two months.

Our UX process was grounded in a mature and evolving persona model, initially centered on seniors but later refined to include low- to mid-literacy users across a broader age range.

UX Audits

Alongside structured work, I maintained a strong personal focus on independent improvement initiatives. I regularly audited the app’s UX and developed a sandboxed “what if...” document filled with ideas for refinement and long-term innovation.

Over time, I noticed areas that could be tightened—navigation felt heavier than it needed to be, some flows ran long, and the text often over-explained instead of guiding. There also wasn’t much hierarchy or emphasis on helping users find what was most relevant to them.

Process

Refining Store Images as a First Step

While improving the initial experience was already on the roadmap, the tone from senior management and the client remained cautious. Since smaller initiatives had already focused on mechanisms to improve App Store ratings—and store images were a recurring topic in client communications—I initiated a proposal to enhance the information displayed. The goal was to positively shape user perception and reinforce their understanding of what the app could offer.

At the same time, an ongoing content initiative had introduced a new service character, casually named "au onesen." The client had been closely involved in refining this character, and with visual design gaining internal traction, I saw an opportunity to incorporate it into the store imagery as a way to strengthen engagement and brand recognition.

Understanding Trends and Commonality

I began by compiling store image designs from a range of popular Japanese apps, looking for recurring patterns and visual trends. I’ve always valued Jakob’s Law—even if it can feel reductive—because it acts as a practical accelerant: by mirroring what users already know, we reduce complexity and ease them into new experiences. For a first-time user flow, that familiarity can be a powerful tool to support smoother onboarding and build early trust.

Key Findings
  1. Scan-First Behavior

    Users tend to focus only on the first two images, scanning quickly rather than reading in depth. This means the top visuals need to communicate value at a glance.

  2. Visuals Over UI

    Key visuals are often used in place of raw UI screenshots, since the UI alone doesn’t provide enough clarity or explanation for users to understand what the app does.

  3. Large, Simplified Text

    Text has become larger and more concise, aiming to explain benefits quickly rather than provide detailed descriptions.

  4. Bold Colors and Human Characters

    Strong color contrasts help capture attention, while the use of human characters adds relatability and warmth to the experience.

Strategy

Validation Through Testing

Following this work, we ran usability testing on both my research-backed design proposal and a more conservative, middle-ground version that aligned more closely with the client’s comfort zone. The sessions were designed to test clarity, visual comprehension, and how well each concept helped users understand what the app offered and how to get started.

The results clearly favored the version I had proposed. Users responded positively to the clearer structure, stronger visual hierarchy, and the way onboarding was surfaced naturally through the visuals.

“It was validating—not just for the execution, but for the thinking behind it. It confirmed that users don’t always need more information; they need better framing and guidance.”

Leveraging Usability testing to improve Provisioning

With the testing results in hand, I saw an opportunity to take the momentum further. My goal wasn’t just to improve the store experience—it was to use that work as a stepping stone into redesigning the provisioning flow. The store visuals were gaining traction internally, and I felt we could build on that energy to introduce a more intentional, visually supported onboarding experience inside the app.

I aligned with PMs and had early support for the direction, but as the conversation moved into real implementation, things slowed. The client leaned toward a more cautious path, and priorities shifted. While the work was well received, it became clear that change would take longer than anticipated.

Provisioning Improvements I Proposed

  1. Visual Onboarding During Provisioning

    Introduce service value clearly during setup using lightweight visuals and messaging tied to the updated store direction.

  2. Renewed Provisioning Flow

    Simplify the initial setup steps, reducing friction and making the experience feel more purposeful.

  3. Content Discoverability

    Guide users to relevant content early, increasing the chances of meaningful first-week engagement.

  4. System Recognition and Personalization

    Collect lightweight inputs to tailor post-provisioning UX based on user interests or goals.

Reflections

Personal Takeaways

This project reminded me that good design work doesn't end at the screen—it also lives in how you communicate ideas, earn trust, and adapt to the pace of a larger organization. While not everything moved forward immediately, seeing clear validation through testing and stakeholder discussion confirmed that the work was headed in the right direction.

It also reinforced the importance of working strategically as a designer—not just clocking off when requirements are met, but thinking ahead, spotting opportunities, and helping shape what comes next. The final design could no doubt be critiqued and refined further, but what mattered most was that the solutions delivered clear, tested improvements. That kind of progress sets a strong foundation for future refinement.

Continued Potential

There’s still plenty of room to extend the thinking behind this work—particularly in improving top-level navigation, simplifying core page layouts into clear, hierarchy-led structures, and surfacing key actions that better align with user intent. These changes could help shape a more intuitive, engaging initial experience.

I also continue to advocate for A/B testing as a complement to usability testing. It offers a faster, lower-friction way to validate concepts in a live environment without disrupting the core user base.

With stronger alignment and prioritization, many of these ideas could still be brought to life in future releases. The foundation is solid—it just needs the right timing and support to move forward.
